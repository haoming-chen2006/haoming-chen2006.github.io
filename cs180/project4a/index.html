A.2: Recovering homographies

One interesting thing I learned when picking points, as shown in the first picture of the appendix, is that you don't need to (in general) pick points all over the image. You can focus on a specific object, and as long as your points are accurate, you achieve the same effect. For instance, in the first appendix image, I tried picking points across the whole image, but it didn't align very well because there isn't much detail there. So I focused on a specific object and got a good result, because there is a lot more detail to align on that object.

We start from this matrix multiplication equation, which maps \((x, y)\) into \((x', y')\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} wx' \\ wy' \\ w \end{bmatrix} \]

From the third row, we find \(w\) to be the following:

\[ gx + hy + 1 = w \]

Next, we substitute \(w\), and we can not care about the third row, and only the first two. So we get these two equations:

\[ ax + by + c = (gx + hy + 1)\,x' \qquad dx + ey + f = (gx + hy + 1)\,y' \]

We're trying to isolate and find \(x'\) and \(y'\), so we moved everything to the left side. So we finally get:

\[ ax + by + c - gxx' - hyx' = x' \qquad dx + ey + f - gxy' - hyy' = y' \] \[ \begin{bmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -xx' & -yx' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -xy' & -yy' \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \\ e \\ f \\ g \\ h \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix} \]

Next, it's just about substituting in the values of the points we found. Each pair of points gives two equations and there are 8 unknowns, so we need at least 4 pairs. With \(n\) pairs, we stack all \(2n\) equations into one system \(Ah = b\):

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x_1x_1' & -y_1x_1' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x_1 & y_1 & 1 & -x_1y_1' & -y_1y_1' \\ & & & & \vdots & & & \\ x_n & y_n & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x_nx_n' & -y_nx_n' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x_n & y_n & 1 & -x_ny_n' & -y_ny_n' \end{bmatrix}}_{A \;(2n \times 8)} \begin{bmatrix} a \\ \vdots \\ h \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_1' \\ y_1' \\ \vdots \\ x_n' \\ y_n' \end{bmatrix}}_{b} \]

Next, for the real numbers, this is the full system \([A \mid b]\) for these 8 pairs:

\[ \left[\begin{array}{rrrrrrrr|r} 597 & 348 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -75819 & -44196 & 127 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 597 & 348 & 1 & -184473 & -107532 & 309 \\ 597 & 472 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -71043 & -56168 & 119 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 597 & 472 & 1 & -263874 & -208624 & 442 \\ 745 & 487 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -209345 & -136847 & 281 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 745 & 487 & 1 & -356855 & -233273 & 479 \\ 745 & 557 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -207855 & -155403 & 279 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 745 & 557 & 1 & -407515 & -304679 & 547 \\ 538 & 710 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -31742 & -41890 & 59 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 538 & 710 & 1 & -376062 & -496290 & 699 \\ 600 & 1085 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -57000 & -103075 & 95 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 600 & 1085 & 1 & -652200 & -1179395 & 1087 \\ 752 & 930 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -201536 & -249240 & 268 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 752 & 930 & 1 & -679808 & -840720 & 904 \\ 712 & 1083 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -169456 & -257754 & 238 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 712 & 1083 & 1 & -772520 & -1175055 & 1085 \end{array}\right] \]

We solve this using least squares (np.linalg.lstsq), which finds the best \(h\), the one that makes the total squared error \(\lVert Ah - b \rVert^2\) as small as possible. This gives the homography:

\[ H = \begin{bmatrix} 1.21876 & -0.04337 & -571.19967 \\ 0.16041 & 1.10860 & -129.22083 \\ 0.00020 & -0.00004 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

A.3: Warping the images

My warping is done in four steps.

  1. Calculate the new image boundary by passing the corners of the old image through our homography matrix.
  2. Use an inverse homography mapping of every pixel in the new image boundary to find where it comes from in the old image.
  3. The difference between nearest neighbor and bilinear: nearest neighbor locates the closest pixel to take its value, while bilinear takes a weighted average of the surrounding pixels it lands in.
  4. Finally, for rectify, I pick the four corners as points and map them to a rectangle with the relevant dimensions: 85.6 × 54 mm for the ID card and roughly 3.7 × 8.5 in for one panel of the folded flyer.

Nearest neighbor vs. bilinear

Honestly, from an overall point of view, there is not a huge difference, especially in time. Both finished quickly, under a second. Bilinear might take a little longer: after timing, I found nearest neighbor takes 0.03 s and 0.07 s on the two images while bilinear takes 0.06 s and 0.14 s, so bilinear actually takes twice as long. The real difference is when zooming in: bilinear generally gives a smoother result, thanks to it smoothing over the surrounding pixels instead of picking one.

A.4: Blending into a mosaic

Since we already have the H for these cases, we're basically doing the same as A.3. We still calculate the boundaries, but we have three images, so we take the maximum boundary out of the 12 calculated corner points together. Next, we apply the same inverse mapping and use bilinear interpolation to find the pixel values.

For the actual blending, I use the suggested alpha idea, where each image's weight is 1 at its center and falls off linearly to 0 at its edges, and take the weighted average of the overlapping images.
