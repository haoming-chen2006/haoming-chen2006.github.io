Project 1

Deciding the measurement

Each glass plate stacks three grayscale exposures — blue on top, green in the middle, red on the bottom. To rebuild the color photograph we hold the blue channel fixed and slide the green and red channels until they line up, then stack the three as an RGB image. "Line up" needs a number that says how well two channels match at a given shift. I use two.

L2 measurement

def measure_l2(a, b):
    return np.sum((a - b) ** 2)

The L2 norm (sum of squared differences) overlays the two channels and adds up the squared difference at every pixel. When the channels are aligned, the same edges and textures sit on top of each other, every difference is small, and the sum is low; when they are off, bright edges land on dark regions and the squared differences blow up. So the best shift is the one that minimizes this number. Squaring removes the sign and punishes big mismatches hard, which sharpens the minimum.

NCC measurement

def measure_ncc(a, b):
    a = a.flatten() - np.mean(a)
    b = b.flatten() - np.mean(b)
    ncc = np.dot(a / np.linalg.norm(a), b / np.linalg.norm(b))
    return -ncc

Normalized cross-correlation flattens each channel to a vector, subtracts its mean, scales it to unit length, and takes the dot product. Two aligned channels point in nearly the same direction, so the dot product is near 1; misaligned ones are closer to orthogonal. Because it subtracts the mean and normalizes the length, NCC is far less sensitive to one channel being globally brighter or higher-contrast than another. It is a similarity (higher is better), so I return its negative and keep the same "lower is better" search as L2.

How alignment uses the measure. The naive aligner tries every integer shift of the channel within a fixed window (±15 px), scores each one with the chosen measure, and keeps the best. Shifts are applied with np.roll. One important detail: the score is computed on the interior of the plate, not the whole thing — the black-and-white scan frame around the edges would otherwise dominate the metric and pull the alignment onto the border instead of the picture, so I crop ~12% off every side before scoring.

Single-scale alignment · low-resolution JPEGs

On the small JPEGs the exhaustive ±15 search is fast enough. L2 and NCC find identical offsets on all three — these are easy, similar-brightness plates where the two metrics are interchangeable.

Multi-scale pyramid · all provided plates

The full-resolution TIFs can't be brute-forced — the true offset runs to well over a hundred pixels, and a window that large at full resolution would be hopelessly slow. Instead the aligner builds an image pyramid: it shrinks both channels by repeated factors of two, finds the shift on the smallest (a wide ±15 search on a tiny image is cheap), scales that estimate up by two, and refines it with a narrow ±2 search at each finer level up to full resolution. Same NCC score, computed on the interior at every level. Every plate aligns in about two seconds.

The one failure: emir. Sixteen of seventeen plates (14 here plus the three selections below) align cleanly; only emir breaks, landing on a nonsense R offset of (483, −461). The reason is brightness: Prokudin-Gorskii's subject wears a bright robe that is very light in the red exposure but nearly black in the blue one, so matching raw intensities — even with NCC — locks onto the wrong place. The standard fix is to align on edges (image gradients) instead of raw pixels, since an edge is an edge in every channel regardless of brightness.

Choosing the pyramid depth

How far down should the pyramid go? Using three_generations (true offset R = (111, 11)) as a test, NCC held fixed:

Medium is the sweet spot. Shallow doesn't downsize enough — its coarsest search reaches only ~60 px, so it stalls at R dy = 66, barely half the true 111, and the result stays fringed. Deep shrinks the coarsest image to a handful of pixels, where the search lands on a meaningless offset of ~−3000; because np.roll wraps around, that happens to alias back near the right shift here, but the number is unusable and on a less forgiving plate it breaks outright. The automatically-chosen middle depth reaches the true offset cheaply, so every result uses it.

Bells & whistles · automatic cropping

Ignoring the border while scoring fixes the alignment, but the finished image still carries the ugly scan frame. auto_crop removes it automatically — no fixed margin. It scans inward from each edge and treats a row or column as frame while it is nearly all black, nearly all white, or nearly flat (no detail), stopping at the first line that looks like real picture, and trims all three channels to that common box.
